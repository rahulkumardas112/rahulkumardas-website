UK Emergency Alert: Why People Are Being Asked to Store Food, Keep Cash at Home and Prepare for 3 Days

The UK's “Be Ready” campaign asks households to take simple steps so they can manage for up to 72 hours if an emergency interrupts everyday services. The message is about practical preparation, not a warning that a specific disaster is imminent.

People across the United Kingdom are being encouraged to keep a modest supply of essential items at home, including drinking water, food that can be stored safely, a torch, a radio and some cash. The advice forms part of the government's “Be Ready” campaign, which focuses on helping households cope if normal services are disrupted.

The central recommendation is straightforward: prepare to manage for three days. Severe weather, flooding, wildfires, power cuts or a cyber incident could temporarily affect transport, phone signals, internet access, water supplies or electronic payments. Having basic necessities ready can make those first hours and days easier to manage.

What is the UK's Be Ready campaign?

The campaign is a public preparedness initiative rather than an emergency order to evacuate or a claim that an attack is about to happen. Updated advice published by the UK government on 22 October 2026 encourages people to take manageable steps in advance. Officials say households that can meet some of their own needs during an interruption can also help emergency services prioritise people who need urgent assistance.

The three day approach is not entirely new. The government says similar advice is used in several European countries, and that much of the UK's household resilience guidance has existed since 2024. The current campaign brings the message to wider public attention.

Why are people being advised to keep cash at home?

Cash is recommended as a backup in case card readers, banking apps, internet connections or cash machines become unavailable. The advice does not mean people should withdraw their savings or keep large amounts of money at home. The appropriate amount depends on a household's likely short term needs, and cash should be stored securely.

Keeping some banknotes and coins alongside bank cards can provide flexibility if electronic payments temporarily fail. The same principle applies to emergency planning more broadly: a small alternative can help when a service people normally rely on is unavailable.

What should households keep for three days?

The official checklist suggests checking what is already at home first and building supplies gradually, rather than buying everything at once. The main items include:

  • Drinking water: the UK checklist suggests three litres per person per day for drinking, or nine litres per person for three days. Additional water may be needed for cooking and washing.
  • Food with a long shelf life: for example, tinned foods, dried fruit and nuts, and other items that do not require refrigeration or extensive cooking.
  • Lighting: a torch and spare batteries. The official guidance notes that torches are safer than candles.
  • Information: a battery powered or wind up FM radio to receive updates if electricity or internet access is disrupted.
  • Phone power: a charged power bank, where suitable for the devices your household uses.
  • Warmth and hygiene: blankets, sleeping bags, warm clothing, hand sanitiser, wet wipes and basic toiletries.
  • Money and essentials: a reasonable amount of cash, bank cards and any essential supplies your family depends on, including regular medication, baby supplies or pet food.
  • Important information: keep useful emergency contacts accessible and consider how you would contact family members if mobile services were unreliable.

Practical tip: Start with what you already have. Add a few missing items during normal shopping, check expiry dates about every six months, and adapt the list to your household's needs. Preparedness should be affordable and gradual.

Is the UK expecting an imminent emergency?

The published guidance presents the campaign as a general resilience measure, not a response to one specific piece of intelligence about an imminent emergency. The risks discussed include extreme weather and incidents that could affect essential services, including cyberattacks. Preparing for disruption is not the same as predicting that it will happen soon.

That distinction matters because dramatic headlines can make routine preparedness advice sound like an immediate crisis warning. The best way to understand the message is to read the official checklist and follow updates from reliable public authorities, rather than relying on rumours or social media claims.

What can readers outside the UK learn from this?

The UK guidance is designed for UK households, and local emergency advice will differ by country and region. Even so, the basic principle is widely useful: think ahead about drinking water, food, medicines, lighting, communication and access to money if normal services are interrupted by a storm, flood or other emergency.

In India, households should follow advisories from local authorities and relevant disaster management agencies, especially during floods, cyclones, heatwaves or other regional emergencies. Preparation should reflect local risks, available space and the needs of older people, children, people with disabilities and pets.

The message: prepare calmly, not fearfully

The three day recommendation is a reminder to make everyday life a little more resilient. It does not require panic buying, excessive stockpiling or keeping large sums of cash at home. A checked cupboard, a torch with working batteries, safe drinking water and a simple family plan can be a sensible start.

Good emergency preparation is less about predicting the future and more about reducing avoidable stress when circumstances change unexpectedly.
